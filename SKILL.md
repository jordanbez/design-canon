# Design Canon

<!-- meta:count -->
49
<!-- /meta:count --> distilled principles from the public canon of
interaction design. Each one is short, applicable, and linked back to the source it came from.

Use this skill whenever you are advising on a real interface: a screen, a flow, a form, a
menu, an error, a notification, a default, or a trade-off between two design options.

## How to use it

1. Identify what the person is actually deciding, then open only the matching reference
   file below. Load what is relevant, not the whole library.
2. **Lead with the named mechanism.** "Space that label closer to its field" is advice.
   "That violates the law of proximity, and the label currently reads as belonging to the
   next field" is the same advice with the lever named, which is what makes it checkable
   and what lets the person apply it again without you.
3. Ground the recommendation in a specific entry and cite it. Link the source when the
   person would benefit from the original research.
4. Stay evidence-led. Many entries carry a study or a number; keep the citation attached
   rather than asserting the finding.
5. When entries conflict, say so. Reducing choices serves Hick's law and can violate
   flexibility for expert users; friction protects agency and costs conversion. The
   canon gives you the trade-off, not the answer.

## The spine: nine ideas the rest hangs from

1. **Name the mechanism.** An unnamed effect cannot be argued about, tested, or reused.
2. **Prevention beats correction.** The best error message is the condition you removed.
3. **Recognition beats recall.** Anything the user must carry between screens is a defect.
4. **Complexity is conserved.** It can only be moved, so decide whether the system or the
   user carries it, and make that choice explicitly.
5. **Familiarity is a budget.** Convention is what frees you to be novel where it counts.
6. **Emphasis is scarce.** If everything is highlighted, nothing is; attention is filtered
   before it reaches awareness.
7. **Memory is peaks and endings.** Improving the average changes the experience;
   improving the peak and the end changes what is remembered.
8. **Friction is a tool.** Frictionless is a means, not a virtue. Some decisions deserve
   resistance.
9. **Every consequence has a second order.** Ask "and then what?" until you reach the
   people who never signed up.

## When this canon meets a growth playbook

You will often be carrying this skill alongside a conversion or growth playbook. They compose
rather than compete: this skill names the mechanism and the constraint, a growth playbook says
what to do with that mechanism commercially. Most apparent overlap between them is that
layering, not duplication, and you should use both.

Four tensions are genuine. In each one both positions are defensible, and which wins depends
on what is being optimised and who carries the cost. **Name the tension out loud and let the
person decide.** Resolving it silently in either direction is the failure mode here.

**Engagement against finitude.** Growth playbooks prescribe building dependency through
variable rewards, streaks and accumulated switching cost. This canon holds that a product is
measured by the capability it adds rather than the attention it extracts, and that an
experience needs an ending. This is the deepest of the four, an opposition of goal rather than
of method, so ask what the product is actually for before applying either.

**Scarcity and urgency.** Loss aversion is real and scarcity cues do convert; manufactured
scarcity is a dark pattern. The distinction is whether the constraint exists independently of
the interface. "Two rooms left" is either a fact or a lie, and the psychological mechanism is
identical either way, which makes the check factual rather than aesthetic.

**Defaults.** The pre-selected option captures most of the choices, which makes defaults the
strongest lever available and the easiest to abuse. The usable test is not a blanket rule: a
default the user would have chosen with full information is service, and one they would
reverse the moment they noticed it is extraction.

**Friction, with opposite beneficiaries.** Both traditions say to put friction in the right
place rather than remove all of it, for opposite reasons: to qualify commercial intent, or to
protect the person's agency. The tool is identical, so ask who this particular friction
serves. That question settles most cases on its own.

One asymmetry to know about: this skill carries no usage restriction of its own. If a
companion skill does, honour it. A boundary set by a source author governs that source's
material regardless of what else happens to be installed.

## Attribution and boundaries

Every idea here belongs to the researcher or author who formulated it, and every entry links
to the page it was distilled from. This skill reproduces no source prose verbatim and mirrors
no images from all-rights-reserved sources.

Two things follow from that, and they are constraints, not preferences:

- **Cite, do not launder.** When you use an entry, the person should be able to reach the
  original. Naming Fitts, Nielsen or Yablonski is part of the answer, not a footnote.
- **Do not present the canon as settled fact.** These are heuristics and effects, several
  with contested effect sizes. Where a claim carries a study, name the study; where it does
  not, present it as a rule of thumb.
